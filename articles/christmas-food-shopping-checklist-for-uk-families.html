Christmas Food Shopping Checklist for UK Families

Start with the guests and meals, check what you have, then split the festive shop into manageable stages.

By Edward Nickless · Fameally Published 21 November 2026 9 minute read

The Christmas dinner ingredients are on the list. Then someone asks what's for breakfast, whether there's anything for the vegetarian guest and who bought the washing-up liquid. A festive food shop has a few more jobs to do than supply one roast.

Start with the people and meals you're actually covering, check what is already at home, then split the shopping into things that can wait in the cupboard and things that need buying nearer the day. The checklist below gives you a starting point to adapt, with an example for six people on Christmas Day and four on Boxing Day.

You can use a piece of paper, a shared note or a shopping-list app. The useful part is having one agreed list that somebody checks before another item goes in the basket.

Confirm the people and meals

Write down who's eating at each meal. Six people for Christmas lunch doesn't necessarily mean six for breakfast or Boxing Day tea. Include overnight visitors, the person arriving after lunch and any meals you're taking to somebody else's home.

Keep the menu short enough to recognise. “Roast dinner, pudding and something easy in the evening” is a more useful starting point than buying several possible starters before anyone has decided whether they want one.

  • Christmas Eve: who's home, and what can you prepare without using the oven space needed for tomorrow?
  • Christmas Day breakfast: familiar breakfast food, milk and hot drinks.
  • The main meal: main dishes, chosen sides, sauces and dessert.
  • The evening: a simple option if people are hungry again.
  • Boxing Day: its own headcount and an easy meal, rather than an assumption that yesterday will supply everything.

Ask guests about allergies and dietary requirements before choosing ingredients. Agree what they can eat and how it needs to be prepared. When shopping, check each product's ingredients and relevant precautionary allergen information, including substitutions; a familiar brand or a vegan label doesn't establish that a product is suitable for a particular allergy. The Food Standards Agency's consumer allergy advice explains the labels and cross-contamination considerations.

Build your Christmas shopping checklist

Copy the groups you need, then replace the examples with your menu. For each item, note the amount required, the usable amount already at home and what remains to buy. If someone else is bringing it, add their name instead of buying a second one.

Main meal and sides

  • Your chosen main: turkey, another roast or a vegetarian alternative, with enough suitable portions for the people eating it.
  • Potatoes and the vegetables you've agreed to serve.
  • Ingredients for the sides you're making, such as stuffing or cauliflower cheese.
  • Gravy ingredients or granules, cooking oil, herbs and seasoning.
  • Sauces and accompaniments that somebody actually wants.
  • Dessert and any accompaniment, such as custard or cream.

Breakfasts and other meals

  • Bread, cereal, oats or your household's usual breakfast food.
  • Milk or suitable alternatives, plus tea and coffee.
  • The ingredients for a straightforward Christmas Eve dinner.
  • A light Christmas evening option and the separate Boxing Day meal.
  • Drinks and snacks in amounts that fit your plans.

Household extras and equipment

  • Washing-up liquid or dishwasher tablets, bin bags and toilet roll.
  • Clean food-storage containers, freezer labels and a pen.
  • Foil or baking paper if your recipes need it.
  • Enough shopping bags, including cool bags for chilled food.
  • A suitable roasting tin and working food thermometer if needed; check what you own before buying equipment.

Open the cupboards while you make this list. Half a jar of cranberry sauce is only useful if its storage instructions and condition still allow you to use it. A bag of potatoes already promised to another dinner isn't spare Christmas stock.

For the everyday items that tend to disappear from memory, use the same check as you would on an ordinary week. Our guide to remembering regular household essentials helps you build that routine.

Work through Christmas for six

Suppose six people are having Christmas lunch: five want the roast and one wants a vegetarian main. Four people will be home for jacket potatoes on Boxing Day. Everyone has agreed the menu and the host has checked ingredients with the vegetarian guest.

These are planning illustrations, not recommended portions or a complete menu. Adjust them for appetites, ages, your recipes and the rest of the meal. Check that the main, gravy, sides and toppings suit the people eating them. Keep any necessary preparation separate.

Notice the distinction between the amount needed and the pack you buy. If carrots come in a larger bag, the list still needs 400 g for this meal. Decide where the extra will go before choosing the pack. The same applies to a roast sold in a larger size than you need.

There is no planned dependence on leftover turkey for Boxing Day. If you later have safely stored leftovers, you can adjust that meal, but the four people eating already have an option. Our guide to turning meals into a shopping list covers combining quantities across several dishes.

Check storage space and dates before buying

Think about where the food will go before adding it to the trolley. A large bird, two desserts and chilled drinks can compete for the same shelf. Include the space needed for safe defrosting and for containers after the meal.

The FSA's Christmas food guidance advises keeping the fridge at 5°C or below, checking it with a thermometer, separating raw food from ready-to-eat food and keeping raw meat covered on the bottom shelf. Follow each product's storage instructions and use-by date. Use cool bags for chilled shopping.

For a frozen turkey, read the pack's defrosting instructions well ahead of the meal and reserve fridge space. A whole bird can take several days. Use the FSA's defrosting guidance for the timing and method, rather than assuming Christmas Eve will be early enough. Food Standards Scotland's festive checklist also advises defrosting meat in the fridge, rather than at room temperature.

Put the storage-dependent jobs on the calendar as well as on the shopping list. “Check turkey instructions and fridge space” belongs before the shop; it is easy to overlook if the only note is “buy turkey”.

Split the shop into useful stages

You don't need a separate trip for every stage. These groups help you decide what can join an ordinary shop and what depends on dates, delivery arrangements or available space.

  1. During November and early December: agree the meals, check equipment, use up existing supplies and buy suitable cupboard items or household extras when convenient.
  2. When freezer space is available: buy the frozen items you have chosen, following their storage instructions. Record what has been bought so another shopper doesn't duplicate it.
  3. Nearer the meals: buy fresh and chilled items with dates that cover the intended use. Check the dates when the shopping arrives as well as when choosing it.
  4. Before the final shop: confirm the headcount and what guests are bringing, then check only the remaining gaps.

If you use a delivery or collection service, check the retailer's current arrangements directly. When an order arrives, compare substitutions with the menu and any dietary requirements. This guide doesn't assume a particular retailer's booking dates, stock or prices.

Give each buying job an owner. “Alex: cupboard items; Sam: final fresh shop” is clearer than both people thinking they might buy everything. If an item moves between those jobs, update the shared list before the next shop.

Do one final household check

Read the meals through in order, from the first breakfast to the last meal you're covering. For each one, ask who is eating, what is being served, whether the ingredients are accounted for and where they will be stored.

  • Are guest numbers and dietary requirements still correct?
  • Have you checked breakfast food, milk and hot drinks as well as dinner?
  • Are gifts of food and dishes brought by guests recorded?
  • Do quantities reflect the meals people will actually eat at home?
  • Are fresh-food dates, fridge space and any defrosting jobs accounted for?
  • Does somebody know which items remain to buy?

After the holiday, keep the notes that will help next time: the side nobody wanted, the breakfast you forgot and the amount you bought too much of. You can fold those into your household master grocery list, while keeping the Christmas-specific choices separate.

Common questions

When should I start the Christmas food shop?
Start with the menu and cupboard check in November or early December. Buy suitable longer-lasting items when convenient, and leave purchases that depend on use-by dates and chilled space until nearer the meals.
How much food do I need for six people?
Count the people eating each dish, then use your recipes and product serving information as a starting point. Adjust for appetites and other courses. The worked table illustrates the shopping calculation; it isn't a universal portion guide.
What is easiest to forget?
Walk through the meals around the main dinner: breakfasts, hot drinks and Boxing Day. Then check the supplies needed to serve, clean up and store food. Your own household history is more useful than buying every item on a generic list.
How do I keep the checklist for shopping?
Copy the groups you need into your usual list, add quantities and cross off the usable food already at home. For a separate paper meal plan, our free resources page has the existing weekly dinner planner.
