How to Plan Meals Around Expiry Dates

Read the labels, choose which food needs using first and give it a workable place in the week.

By Edward Nickless · Fameally Published 9 November 2026 8 minute read

The chicken is dated Tuesday. A sauce was opened yesterday. There are peas in the freezer and pasta in the cupboard. They can all help with dinner, but they don't all need your attention at the same time.

Planning meals around expiry dates starts by reading the actual label. Put food with a short safe-use window into a meal you can realistically make, then build the rest of the week around it. A date on its own isn't enough: storage, opening instructions and what has happened to the food matter too.

You don't need to catalogue the whole kitchen. Start with a few chilled ingredients and anything you've already opened. A short note beside the meal plan is enough to keep the next decision visible.

Separate use-by and best-before dates

“Expiry date” is a useful everyday phrase, but UK food labels make an important distinction. A use-by date concerns safety. A best-before date concerns quality. Treating both as the same deadline can mean throwing away usable food or taking a risk with something that should no longer be eaten.

  • Use-by: use the food by that date while following its storage instructions. Don't eat it afterwards, even if it looks or smells normal.
  • Best-before: passing the date doesn't automatically make food unsafe. Check its condition and follow storage instructions and any advice specific to that food.

The UK government's date-label guidance explains the distinction. The FSA also explains why a sniff test cannot establish the safety of food past its use-by date. Read the wording on the particular pack rather than assuming all products of the same type use the same label.

For planning, this means a chilled ingredient reaching its use-by date deserves attention before a properly stored packet of dried pasta approaching its best-before date. You still need to decide whether that ingredient fits a meal someone will actually cook.

Check opening and storage instructions

A printed date isn't the only instruction. An opened product may say to refrigerate it and use it within a particular time. Write down when you opened it, especially if another person will prepare the next meal. Follow both the opening instructions and the use-by date; use whichever limit comes first.

For example, a pack may still have several days left on its printed date, while its instructions require it to be used within two days of opening. Opening it doesn't give you permission to keep it until the later printed date. The government's label guidance also explains that storage instructions apply after opening.

Keep the fridge between 0°C and 5°C and check it with a thermometer. Keep raw meat in sealed packaging or a clean, sealed container on the bottom shelf, away from ready-to-eat food. These storage steps matter even when a date hasn't passed; see the Food Standards Agency's chilling guidance.

If you don't know when a product was opened or whether it was stored safely, a neat place in the meal plan won't resolve that uncertainty. Check the relevant food-safety guidance before using it.

Give priority food a realistic meal

Make a short list of food needing a decision. Beside each item, note its usable quantity, the relevant instruction and what you intend to do. “Chicken: Tuesday dinner” is more useful than a general reminder to use up meat.

  1. Choose the food that needs attention first. Read the whole label, including anything about opening, freezing or cooking.
  2. Choose a familiar meal that uses it. Check the other ingredients and how many people it will feed.
  3. Choose an evening with enough time. Put a quick dinner on the late night and a longer recipe where someone can cook it.
  4. Make an earlier decision if that evening won't work. Check whether the food can be frozen safely, or change the meal before its safe-use window closes.

There is no benefit in assigning a time-consuming recipe to a night when everyone gets home late. Our guide to making a weekly meal plan that survives a normal UK week helps match dinners to the time available.

Keep this date check distinct from the broader job of planning around food you already have. That routine finds useful starting ingredients; this one decides which of them needs using first.

Decide what to cook or freeze before the deadline

When a meal moves, freezing may give you another option. Check that the food is suitable and follow its freezing instructions. The FSA says suitable food can be frozen on its use-by date; don't leave the decision until after that date has passed. Freezing doesn't make unsafe food safe.

Label the food with its contents, portions and freezing date. Put a reminder in the plan for the preparation it will need later. The FSA advises defrosting in the fridge and using food within 24 hours of full defrosting; follow the product's instructions too. See its freezing and defrosting guidance for the full process.

Cooking creates another storage decision. For ordinary cooked leftovers, cool and refrigerate within one to two hours, then eat within 48 hours or freeze them if that won't be possible. Reheat only once, until steaming hot throughout. Follow the FSA's leftovers guidance and any shorter instructions for the specific food. Cooked rice, for example, needs quicker cooling and a shorter fridge-storage period.

If you cook Tuesday's chicken and save some for another meal, record when it was cooked and stored. Don't carry the original raw-food label forward as if it describes the cooked leftovers.

Put the decision into the week

Use the plan to remember the meal

After checking the labels, add the chosen dinner to your weekly plan in Fameally. Keep the date and opening checks on the packaging or in a simple household note, so the person cooking can check them again.

Recheck when the week changes

If Tuesday becomes an evening out, deal with Tuesday's ingredients as well as moving the dinner. Check the chicken's label while there is still time to act. Bring that meal forward, safely freeze suitable food or make another safe decision based on its instructions.

A replacement dinner should be easy to keep available: a cupboard meal or suitable freezer option can cover an unexpected late finish. That gives you flexibility without relying on chilled food surviving longer than its instructions allow.

When somebody else cooks or shops, make the handover specific. “The sauce was opened Sunday evening; Monday's pasta uses it” is more useful than “there's some sauce to use up”. For the wider buying, storage and leftover routine, see our practical family guide to reducing food waste.

Before you finish the plan

  • Read use-by, best-before and opening instructions separately.
  • Check storage conditions and note when opened food needs using.
  • Assign priority ingredients to meals someone can cook in time.
  • Make suitable freezing decisions before the safe-use window closes.
  • Recheck the food whenever a meal moves.

A few common questions

Should I always cook the item with the earliest printed date?
Check the type of date and any opening instructions first. An opened chilled ingredient may need attention before an unopened cupboard food carrying an earlier best-before date.
Can I use a smell test after a use-by date?
No. Food can contain harmful bacteria even when it looks and smells normal. Follow the use-by date and storage instructions.
Can I freeze something on its use-by date?
The FSA allows suitable food to be frozen on that date, provided it has been stored safely. Check its freezing instructions and act before the date passes.
Do I need an expiry-tracking app?
A short manual note can support this method. Read the pack, record opening times when needed and put the chosen meal into the plan. A shopping suggestion doesn't replace that check.
